Garmin metrics explained

The watch shows dozens of numbers and says almost nothing about where they come from. We take them apart from primary sources: Garmin's own manuals, Firstbeat documentation and the research where a metric was tested against a lab. With quotes — and with a mark wherever the published part ends and our own explanation begins.

Explainer 04 · VO2 max.

VO2 max. on a watch: low for the strong, high for the rest

Two independent lab validations agree where their samples overlap: above 55 ml/kg/min the watch reads 3–6 ml/kg/min low, and one of them found it reads about 7% high below 45. On the Cooper Institute table that is one or two rungs, so the error shows up less in the figure than in the word beside it. Explainer 11 · Sleep score

Sleep score and sleep efficiency: two numbers about one night

A score of 64 out of 100 next to 93% sleep efficiency isn't a glitch — the two measure different things. The score is a composite of three parts Garmin names but never weighs, and the third of them, overnight recovery, is why it reacts to your evening. Efficiency is a plain share you can check by hand. Firstbeat's own validation puts recognition of sleep at 94% and of wakefulness at 63%, which is why the share on your screen is overstated below about 86% — and the worse the night, the more.

Explainer 01 · Training effect

Training effect: why your hardest session only maintains

The score after a workout is calculated, not measured, and its reference point is the maximum heart rate in your profile — 220 minus your age until you change it, a formula whose standard error of estimate runs about 7 to 12 beats per minute. Set too high, it quietly lowers every score; set too low, it raises them. The bands and labels from the manual, which work raises the aerobic score and which the anaerobic one, why training effect and training status are not the same metric although both can say "maintaining", and the four other sources of drift Firstbeat names.

Explainer 15 · Recovery time

Recovery time: 52 hours is until the next hard session, and the number will move

The countdown reads like a two-day sentence and isn't one: Garmin's definition names the next hard workout and says nothing at all about easy work. Nor is the number fixed at the finish — the watch recalculates it all day from sleep, stress, relaxation and activity, so morning belongs next to morning. The ceiling is published — four days — but in the glances table rather than on the section's own page, which is why it is missed. What was never published: the bottom of the scale and how training load becomes hours.

Explainer 16 · HRV status

Garmin HRV status: unbalanced can mean above your range — and that is not a compliment

Everyone knows one thing about this metric: no status for three weeks. True of the status, not of the screen — the nightly number and the five-minute chart are promised from the first days, the seven-day average after the seventh night. Then the part fewer people know: unbalanced means your average has left your personal range in either direction, and abnormally high values relative to that baseline are something Garmin itself ties to overreach, especially when built out of large amounts of easy work. The four statuses and the asymmetric scale, why the range disappears from the screen on "poor" by design, what is actually being measured, and what Garmin never published.

Explainer 17 · Training load

Garmin training load: 0.8–1.4 is published, the acute load scale has no numbers at all

Your watch says your load is High, and there is no way to find out what High means: the manuals we checked never put a single number on that scale. One gauge over, the ratio is specified to one decimal place — 0.8 to 1.4 optimal, 1.5 and up High. Of the three metrics in this explainer, only the ratio comes with numbers — and our read is that this is not generosity. Three different definitions of acute load (two of them in the same manual), the seven days that vanished from the wording, chronic load with no definition at all, where the popular "28 days" actually came from, and a testable consequence: a session with no heart rate does not fully count.

Explainer 18 · Race predictions

Garmin race predictions: how accurate they are, and how to measure it on yourself

For methods that turn a single fitness number into a finish time, the error follows the runner's level: across 7,663 Valencia finishers the Daniels table missed by 1.11% for those who broke 2:30 and by 17.73% for those finishing between 5:30 and 6:00 — under two minutes against more than an hour, in one and the same race. Those percentages belong to the table, not to the watch: nobody has checked Garmin's marathon prediction against a marathon. What the manuals say, the three lines that have vanished from them beside the prediction — including a blunt "this presumes you have completed the proper training for the race" — and how to measure your own miss in a single tune-up race.
