Stress on a Garmin watch: what the number means, and why it vanishes during hard training

You finish the intervals, open the Stress glance — and where the hardest hour of your day should be, there's nothing at all. It isn't a glitch: the manual says outright that when you're too active for the watch to read your stress, the measurements are not recorded. And the number you do see is doing more work than it looks. The one most people scroll past, by our reading, quietly feeds the two they do look at: stress history for the last three days is one of the seven inputs of Training Readiness, and stress level is one of the four inputs of Body Battery™. On top of that, the word "stress" covers two different metrics on the watch, with a third standing next to them on the same foundation: heart rate variability. A breakdown from the Garmin manuals — how to tell all three apart, how to read the 0–100 scale, and what to do with your own number.

Short answer: the reading vanishes during hard work because it isn't taken then — stress is calculated while you're inactive. It's high while you're doing nothing for the same reason: those calm minutes are the only ones it's measured in, and what you're looking at may be yesterday's session or a short night rather than this moment. The bands — 0–25, 26–50, 51–75, 76–100 — are, by our reading, a general scale and not your norm, so one reading on its own says very little. Our advice: seven days of values with the hard days marked next to them is about ten minutes of work, and it's what turns the number into something you can act on. The five steps are below.

The word "stress" covers two different metrics

Garmin puts two different things under the word "stress": stress level and the HRV stress test. A third lives next to them — HRV status — where the word "stress" doesn't appear but the foundation is the same. All three are built on heart rate variability, that is, on how the intervals between heartbeats change. Whether it's one and the same measure in all three cases, the manuals don't say.

Stress level: how to read the scale

The definition is short: the device analyzes heart rate variability while you are inactive and turns it into a scale from 0 to 100.

Your device analyzes your heart rate variability while you are inactive to determine your overall stress. The stress level range is from 0 to 100, where 0 to 25 is a resting state, 26 to 50 is low stress, 51 to 75 is medium stress, and 76 to 100 is a high stress state.

Why there's no stress reading during hard training

This is stated outright, and the wording is blunter than you'd expect: during high activity the measurements aren't merely hidden — they aren't recorded.

Displays your current stress level and a graph of your stress level. If you are too active for the watch to determine your stress level, stress measurements are not recorded.

fēnix 8 Owner's Manual, edition v10, Glances, the Stress row

Our reading: Garmin doesn't name where the "too active" threshold sits. During hard work a gap is expected; on a calm walk the readings may well be recorded.

Two practical consequences.

First: a gap in the daily graph is a condition of the metric, not a fault. The minutes the watch judged too active never entered the calculation; there's no lost data to go looking for there. By "daily" we mean the 24-hour graph you see in the app; what period the glance on the watch draws, the manual doesn't name.

Second: the number you see walking out of the gym doesn't measure how hard the session was. It was taken afterwards, once you'd settled. To judge how hard the session was, look at the session itself and at training load — that's our advice, not a Garmin rule.

Garmin doesn't publish how a workout that finished half an hour ago affects a reading taken at rest. Our assumption: a reading taken right after a hard effort deserves more caution than a calm evening one — otherwise the aftermath of training is easy to mistake for something else. The order of the steps below is built on that same assumption.

Why your stress is high when you're doing nothing

The question sounds like a complaint about the watch, but it actually describes how the metric works: it's calculated in exactly those minutes — while you are inactive. Our reading: a high value at rest doesn't by itself mean something bad is happening right now.

Per the vívosmart 3 manual, stress level is affected by training, physical activity, sleep, nutrition and general life stress; the manual gives no weights for those factors. Our reading: a high value in the morning can therefore be the aftermath of yesterday's work or a short night as easily as it can be today. One number can't pick from that list, but a week of readings can — if the high values turn up the day after hard sessions and not otherwise, the cause is visible without any formula.

How the HRV stress test and HRV status differ

The HRV stress test is a separate one-off reading, not a daily feed. To see it at all you need a chest strap.

Before you can view your heart rate variability (HRV) stress score, you must put on a Garmin chest heart rate monitor and pair it with your device.

Forerunner 935 Owner's Manual, HRV Stress Test

The test takes three minutes, standing still, and the scale is 1 to 100. Garmin doesn't say what a given number means: there are no bands here as there are for daily stress. The consequence is simple: per this manual you can't get the test from the wrist, its number can't be compared with daily stress, and it only makes sense within a series of your own tests taken the same way — same time of day, same posture, same state beforehand. That's our advice, not a Garmin rule.

HRV status is taken overnight and shows one of four categories. The watch reads wrist heart rate during sleep and compares your seven-day average against your baseline range (for the Poor category, against the normal range for your age).

The status won't appear until three weeks of consistent sleep data have accumulated — the HRV status page says it in a plain sentence. What "well below" means in figures, and where the age norms come from, isn't published. And Garmin's wording here is careful: a balanced status "may indicate positive signs of health", while an unbalanced or poor one "may be a sign of fatigue, greater recovery needs, or increased stress" — may, not does. Our reading: that caution is worth borrowing for your own conclusions.

So stress level and HRV status rest on one foundation — heart rate variability — and look at it from different sides. The first takes it while you're inactive and answers "how are things now", although what window that "now" covers isn't named. The second collects it across a night and a week and answers "how has your last week gone against your own baseline". They don't have to agree — the definitions are different, and a disagreement between them isn't in itself a sign that the device is wrong.

Is the stress level accurate?

Garmin publishes no accuracy check of its own. Independent ones exist, and they aren't flattering. A preregistered lab study (Stress and Health, 2025) put the vívosmart 4 against a chest strap: the stress score did separate a stress task from rest, and it tracked variability measures taken from the strap — but as a predictor of how stressed people said they felt it was marginal, weaker than plain heart rate. A field study of 781 students wearing the same model (Journal of Psychopathology and Clinical Science, 2025) found that for most of them the watch's stress and their own reports of stress had very weak to no association at all.

Read that for what it says: the number follows something real in the body, and it is a poor guess at what you are feeling. What is published about the calculation is the input and the four bands. The averaging window, the thresholds inside a band and the measure of variability used are not. Our conclusion: read the number as a deviation from your own usual level and set it against how you feel, not as a measurement of your state with a known error. That's the whole reason the procedure below is built on a stretch of days: a value you can't check against a reference, you can still check against yourself.

What to do with your own number

One evening of work, then ten minutes a week. Screen names follow the fēnix 8 glances list.

  1. Open the Stress glance and look at the day, not the number. The glance shows a current level and a graph. Write down two things: which band you spend most of your calm hours in, and where the gaps in the graph are. A gap during hard training is expected; a gap during calm hours, by our reading, means the watch was off your wrist or couldn't take a reading. If the graph is full of holes most of the day, our advice: check that the strap sits snugly and that you wear the watch consistently.
  2. Collect seven days in a row in Garmin Connect™. It stores a daily stress level and long-term trends. Take seven consecutive daily values: one day won't show you a norm, seven will. How the daily level is derived from the individual readings, the manual doesn't say. Our reading: it's one number for the whole day, and you can't pick the hours inside it. If you want to compare like with like, open the stress graph for the day, if your model shows one, and take a calm evening stretch rather than the time right after a session.
  3. Mark the hard workouts and short nights next to it. That's the analysis. If the high values land on the days after hard work or after a short night — our hypothesis: that's the cause, and it's tested by backing off. If the values sit flat and have no relation to training, then by our reading training doesn't explain them: look at sleep and at how you feel, and if it holds for a couple of weeks, see a doctor.
  4. Check it against the night side — the HRV status glance. It answers for a week, not for one night, so read it together with the seven days from step 2. If the status hasn't appeared yet, that's normal — it arrives after three weeks of consistent sleep; do the step without it. If they agree (daily stress above your usual and the status out of its baseline range), the signal is solid; if they disagree, trust the seven days over one daily reading. Neither of those rules comes from the manual — both are ours.
  5. Put the conclusion in one sentence and name a date. Something like: "my usual values are such and such, right now it's been higher for this many days, it lines up with this, and I'm watching until this date." The last part isn't optional: without a date by which you expect it back, the observation never ends in a decision. What to do if it hasn't come back by then is in the coach's hypothesis below.

And the reverse move, which is what all this is for. If Garmin showed low Training Readiness this morning and you can't see why, open stress for the last three days: it's one of the seven inputs of the score. Our reading: a calm week of stress readings alongside low readiness means the cause is worth looking for among the other inputs; three high days in a row may be part of the answer. Garmin doesn't publish how much the input weighs, so this is a direction to search in, not a calculation.

What not to do: hunt for a hard workout in the daily graph (it most likely isn't there), compare the HRV stress test number against daily stress (different scales, different sensors), or fold all three into one "overall stress picture" — different windows and scales give you a number with nothing behind it.

A coach's hypothesis, not a device verdict: if high values hold for several days in a row during calm hours — not straight after a workout — and hard training days are running alongside, it looks like you've overreached and aren't recovering in time. Drop one or two hard sessions and see whether the values come down within a week or two. If they don't, or you notice something else alongside — sleep, weight, mood — see a doctor: training isn't the only thing that causes this.
