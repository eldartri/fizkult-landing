The table is built from distance alone and knows nothing about hills, wind or heat. On a rolling course, even numbers mean you are working harder on every climb — and the bill for that arrives in the last third. Hence the working rule: hold effort rather than pace on the climbs, and take the seconds back on the descents instead of grinding out the graph.

What the number means

Pace goes up when you run slower. It is the one value on this screen that runs backwards: 8:00 min/mile is faster than 8:30. That is why pace and speed sit side by side here — 8:00 min/mile and 7.5 mph are the same number turned the other way round.

Target pace is the exam, not the syllabus. Shape is built by easy volume, which runs slower than target pace, and by short intervals, which run faster; target pace on its own trains almost nothing, because you can neither accumulate volume at it nor get real quality out of it. One number does not make a plan — the shape you are in does.

An even graph is a reference, not a race plan. At the half and the full, people usually build in a margin: the first half goes 30–60 seconds slower than the even graph, the second half the same amount faster. The point is to save something for the second half, where the fatigue usually turns up. The split field above builds that whole table for you, so you are not holding the offset in your head at mile nine.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert running pace to speed?
Speed in miles per hour is 60 divided by your pace in minutes per mile. An 8:00 min/mile pace is 7.50 mph; 10:00 min/mile is exactly 6 mph; 6:00 min/mile is 10 mph. It works the same way backwards: 60 divided by your speed. The Fizkult pace calculator shows both at once so you never have to do it in your head: pace is the number you think in outdoors, speed is the number on the treadmill console.

What pace do I need to break four hours in the marathon?
Exactly four hours over 26.2 miles is 9:09 min/mile, or 6.55 mph. To leave yourself a margin, most people plan for 3:57–3:58, which is 9:02–9:05 min/mile. Put Marathon and your target time into the Fizkult pace calculator and it will give you the pace and lay the distance out mile by mile, and the negative split field will rebuild that table around a plan where the first half gives seconds away and the second half takes them back. But the pace you finish on and the pace you train at are two different things: a plan aimed at a result is built from the shape you are in, not from the number you want.

What is a negative split and how do I calculate one?
A negative split means the second half of the distance is faster than the first. You do not have to work it out in your head: put 30 seconds into the negative split field and the Fizkult pace calculator rebuilds the whole table — the first half comes out 30 seconds slower than the even graph, the second half the same amount faster, and the finish time stays where it was. A 1:45 half marathon split evenly is 52:30 for each half; with a 30-second split it is 53:00 and 52:00.

Why does my watch show a different pace than the calculator?
Usually because the watch measured a different distance. The calculator works from the exact number — 26.2188 miles for a marathon — while the watch builds the track out of GPS points, and in woods, between buildings and around switchbacks it collects more points than your legs covered, so the screen at the finish reads a few hundred meters long. The watch divides your average pace by its own distance, which makes that pace look better than the real one. What to do about it on race day: run the course markings and check your time at the official mile markers rather than watching the average pace on your wrist. Where else the numbers on a watch part company with the obvious reading is what our Garmin metrics series is about.

Is the pace a pace calculator gives me my training pace?
No. The Fizkult pace calculator is arithmetic: it converts one number into another and knows nothing about you. Target pace is what gets tested on race day, not what builds the shape you race in: the easy volume that builds it runs slower than target pace and the short intervals run faster, and you need both halves. Turning a target pace into a plan takes the shape you are actually in — what you have been running these last weeks, how you sleep and how you recover. That is no longer a calculator, that is a coach's job.
