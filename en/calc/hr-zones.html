Where 208 − 0.7 × age and 220 − age come from

Nobody ever derived the second formula. Tanaka, Monahan and Seals traced its origin in 2001: it came out of reviews from the seventies, where it was set — their word — arbitrarily, on about a dozen studies with almost nobody over 55. They themselves gathered 351 studies, 492 groups and 18,712 people and arrived at 208 − 0.7 × age; separately, in the lab, they cross-validated it on 514 people aged 18 to 81 and got practically the same line. It leans on neither sex nor training status: trained and untrained people produced the same slope.

And the most important number on this page is not the formula but the scatter around it. Among people of the same age, measured maximum heart rate scatters around the line with a standard deviation of 7 to 11 beats. That is larger than the distance between the two formulas in mid-life: the argument about which of them is right is smaller than the difference between two people of the same age. Ten beats of error in the maximum move every boundary by 5 to 10 beats — a quarter to a half of a band's width on the max scale, and the reserve bands are narrower still, so the same error weighs even more there. That is enough to push a run that was close to a boundary into the next band. Which is why the authors recommend measuring the maximum directly whenever that is possible, and keeping the formula as a starting point.

The two formulas do not disagree everywhere. They cross at around forty, and for a runner between thirty and fifty the difference is no more than three beats. It becomes noticeable at the edges: about four beats at 25, six at 60, nine at 70, and more than 20 for some older adults. The calculator prints both numbers side by side so that this is shown rather than retold.

Why one person has two zone tables

The “by max” scale counts up from zero beats. Seventy percent of 184 is 129, and it makes no difference whether your heart does 45 or 70 at rest. But a living person has no zero: even lying down the heart is working, and the bottom of the range is permanently occupied. Martti Karvonen and colleagues proposed a different count in 1957 — from the reserve: take the gap between resting and maximum heart rate and measure the share inside that gap. At a resting heart rate of 50 the same seventy percent gives 144 beats, fifteen higher; at a resting heart rate of 70 it gives 150, twenty-one higher. The gap between the scales equals the resting heart rate multiplied by the share still missing to one: it grows the higher the resting heart rate and the lower the zone.

This is where all the confusion with numbers comes from. The same run easily turns out to be zone two on one scale and zone three on the other — same person, same effort, different ruler. The Forerunner 945 manual allows zones to be built four ways: in beats, as a share of maximum heart rate, as a share of heart rate reserve, or as a share of threshold heart rate. On top of that, different apps use a different number of zones. So the question “what is my zone two” has no answer without naming the scale, while “what percentage of maximum is this” always does.

And the slicing into ten-percent bands is itself a convention, not physiology. Nowhere in the body is it written that something happens at 79 percent of maximum and something else at 81. Approaches that build zones from threshold heart rate put the boundaries elsewhere and arrive at a different number of zones; the reserve method is likewise a way of counting, proposed in 1957, not a measured law. It is the same thought as the one about numbers, one level deeper: the scale is a tool, not a property of a person.

The Fizkult workout breakdown is built the same way: it shows how many minutes your heart rate spent in each band — when enough heart rate was recorded to count that — and labels the bands by their boundary, “70–80%”, rather than by a number. A number there would belong to your watch settings, not to you.

What to do with all this

The uncertainty worth closing is the measured maximum, not the resting heart rate. An easy run will not show it: the maximum appears where the work ends. In the field it is usually looked for like this: warm up properly, then run two or three minutes up a long hill at full effort, putting everything left into the last thirty seconds — and take the peak on your watch at the end as your number, provided it does not look like a jump of a couple of seconds and it matched the feeling of the limit. A doubtful peak is worth rechecking with a chest strap or repeating on another day: wrist optics go wrong most often on exactly this kind of maximal work. The second chance arrives on its own: the final kilometer of a 5K or 10K race often comes very close to the maximum. And two caveats that matter more than the protocol itself. This is work to exhaustion — it is not for anyone just starting out or coming back after a break or an illness, and not for anyone whose doctor has mentioned their heart or their blood pressure: for them the maximum is determined on a supervised test, not on a hill. And you stop by what you feel, not by the stopwatch: pain or pressure in the chest, dizziness, a skipping heartbeat, breathlessness out of proportion to the effort — reason to stop at once and see a doctor, not to grind out the last thirty seconds.

The column to use is the one your watch builds its zones on. Otherwise your plan and your screen will call the same run by different zone numbers, and the argument will never end. Which one you are on is visible in your heart rate zone settings. The second column is not spare, though — it shows how much the number depends on the chosen scale rather than on you, and that is precisely the reason not to get attached to the number.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate maximum heart rate from age?
Multiply your age by 0.7 and subtract the result from 208 — that is the Tanaka, Monahan and Seals formula from 2001, derived from 351 studies and cross-validated in the lab on 514 people aged 18 to 81. At forty it gives 180 beats. The Fizkult heart rate zone calculator does the arithmetic for you and lays the result out in bands straight away. But the authors put a caveat next to the formula that matters more than the formula itself: among people of the same age, measured maximum heart rate scatters around it with a standard deviation of 7 to 11 beats, which is why they recommend measuring it directly whenever that is possible.

How do reserve zones differ from max heart rate zones?
In where the counting starts. Max zones take a share of maximum heart rate, which means counting up from zero beats — a number no living person has. Reserve zones take a share of the gap between resting and maximum heart rate; that is the Karvonen method from 1957. For the same person the second table comes out above the first, and the higher the resting heart rate, the further apart they move: the gap at every boundary equals the resting heart rate multiplied by the share still missing to one. That is why the same run lands in zone two on one scale and zone three on the other — the calculator prints both columns side by side so that it is visible at once.

Can you trust zones calculated from a formula?
As a starting point yes, as a measurement no, and the reason is arithmetic rather than caution. Why do different apps give me different zones?
Because a zone is not a property of a person but a division of a scale, and the app picks the scale. The Forerunner 945 manual allows zones to be built four ways: in beats, as a share of maximum heart rate, as a share of heart rate reserve, or as a share of threshold heart rate. On top of that, the number of zones differs from app to app: the table in that same manual has five, other programs do it differently. The zone number changes when the scale changes, while a share of maximum stays a share of maximum — which is why Fizkult labels bands in its workout breakdown by their boundary rather than by a number: a run at 165 beats with a maximum of 184 is 90 percent of maximum in any system, but its zone number is different in each.
