Where 18.5 and 25 come from

The formula itself contains nothing but two numbers. Weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared — that is how the WHO defines the index, and neither sex nor age nor way of life enters it. Everything else — the bands, the names, the thresholds — was hung on that fraction afterwards and has nothing to do with the arithmetic.

The "normal weight" band is wider than it sounds. The WHO draws its lines at 18.5, 25.0 and 30.0; at 5 ft 10 in that works out to 129 lb, 174 lb and 209 lb. So normal weight at a single height is stretched across forty-five pounds: two people of that height, one at 132 lb and one at 174 lb, both fall inside it — and the two bodies differ so much you could not confuse them. That is exactly why the table on this page converts the bands into pounds: in index units the width is unreadable, and in pounds it reads instantly.

And the boundaries themselves are round numbers on a continuous scale. Nothing happens in a body between 24.9 and 25.0; it is one and the same point that landed on opposite sides of a line. The WHO prints a caveat next to its bands that rarely gets quoted: the index may not correspond to the same degree of fatness across different populations, partly because of different body proportions. That is said about populations, not about an individual.

What the index doesn't know

It cannot tell what the weight is made of. A kilogram of muscle and a kilogram of fat enter the formula identically, because there is no term in it that would separate them. For a runner that sounds harmless; for a rower or a track cyclist it is not: mass built by work pushes the index up in exactly the same way fat does.

How far off it goes was once measured on athletes directly. In 2011 Etchison and colleagues gathered school sports physicals from Georgia and Alabama covering 1985 to 2003: 33,595 people aged 11 to 19, each with both an index and a caliper measurement. The index called 13.31 percent obese; the calipers called 5.95 percent. Of those the index flagged, 62 percent were not confirmed by the calipers. The error hardly went the other way at all: of those the index did not flag, 99 percent were not flagged by the calipers either. The authors' conclusion fits in one line: BMI is a measurement of relative body weight, not body composition. These were adolescents, so the exact proportion cannot be carried across to an adult athlete one for one — it has the direction right, and the figure is worth holding as an order of magnitude (the objections raised to this study are in source 4).

And it doesn't know where the weight sits. The same number, two different bodies: one carries that weight all over, the other at the waistline. For risk those are different stories, and this is exactly where the second measure goes in.

The second measure: waist to height

The NHS rule is one line long: keep your waist to less than half your height. You can divide in whatever units you like — inches or centimetres — and the ratio comes out the same. Handbooks summarising the British guideline NG246 split it into three bands: 0.4 to 0.49 is the healthy range, 0.5 to 0.59 increased, 0.6 and above high.

What it is good for is exactly where the index is blind. It looks not at how much weight there is but at where it lies. By those same NG246 summaries it is applied across both sexes, across all ethnic groups and including adults with high muscle mass. For an athlete that is the whole reason to add a second line: a measure taken at the waist rather than on the scales.

It has limits of its own. From an index of 35 up these bands are not used — in that case the calculator prints the ratio but names no band. The rule is not meant for children under five, for pregnancy, for eating disorders or for growth disorders. And separately: neither of these two numbers diagnoses anything. If weight is changing on its own, with no change in food or training, give it a couple of weeks and see whether it comes back; if it doesn't, or something else joins it, from sleep to race results, see a doctor.

What to do with all this

The sharpest statement here was not made by us. In 2025 The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology convened a commission of 58 experts, its conclusions were endorsed by 76 organisations, and its summary table records this: BMI-based measures should be used only as a surrogate measure of health risk at a population level, and they provide inadequate information about health at the individual level. To speak of excess body fat in a particular person the commission requires either a direct measurement of fat or at least one additional anthropometric measure alongside the index — and with two such measures the index is not needed at all. It notes separately that above 40 excess fat can pragmatically be assumed without further measurement, but that is a practical allowance rather than one more threshold.

And for someone who trains, a different number matters more — not today's, but yesterday's set beside today's. A single weigh-in says little: in our own measurements, of 186 days with a recorded weight 22 carried more than one weigh-in, and the gap between the first and the last within a day ran to 1.3 kg; food, water and time of day are the usual explanation. Meaning appears in a series: where weight has moved, under what load and what sleep. No calculator works that out, because it does not have your history.

This is a coach's reading, not a medical one. If weight is changing by itself, or something else is worrying you, see a doctor.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate BMI?
Divide weight in kilograms by height in metres squared — that is the WHO definition word for word, and there is nothing else in the formula. In imperial units it is the same arithmetic after a conversion: 5 ft 10 in and 163 lb comes out at 23.4. Age, sex and training history are not inputs; the index knows nothing about you beyond those two numbers. The Fizkult BMI calculator does the arithmetic and also shows the corridor of weight that falls inside each WHO band at your height.

What BMI counts as normal?
The WHO calls 18.5 to 24.9 normal weight, below 18.5 underweight, 25.0 and above overweight, 30.0 and above obesity. The top of the band is printed as 24.9 because it has been rounded to one decimal; the boundary itself is a single number, 25, and it belongs to the band above. The more useful fact is the width: at 5 ft 10 in, normal weight runs from 129 to 174 lb — a spread of forty-five pounds. Two people of that height, one at 132 lb and one at 174 lb, both land inside it, and those two bodies look nothing alike.

Why does BMI call an athlete overweight?
Because the index measures relative weight, not body composition — that is the wording of the authors of a study of 33,595 school athletes. Muscle is heavier than the same volume of fat, and mass built by training raises the index exactly as fat does: the formula has no term that separates them. In the 2011 study by Etchison and colleagues, those athletes aged 11 to 19 in Georgia and Alabama were measured both ways: BMI called 13.31 percent of them obese, skinfold calipers 5.95 percent, and of those BMI flagged, 62 percent were not confirmed by the calipers. They were adolescents, and the exact percentages have been disputed, so hold them as an order of magnitude — the mechanism does not change. Hence the second measure on this page: waist-to-height is measured at the waist, not on the scales.

What is waist-to-height ratio?
Waist circumference divided by height, both measured in the same units — inches or centimetres, the ratio comes out the same. The NHS rule is one line long: keep your waist to less than half your height. Reference handbooks summarising the British guideline NG246 split that into three bands: 0.4 to 0.49 is the healthy range, 0.5 to 0.59 increased, 0.6 and above high. What makes the measure worth adding is precisely where the index goes blind: it looks at where the weight sits rather than how much of it there is. By those same summaries it is applied across both sexes, all ethnic groups and adults with high muscle mass. One caveat matters: from a BMI of 35 up these bands are not used.

How do you measure your waist correctly?
Not at the belt and not at the narrowest point. The NHS describes it like this: find the bottom of your ribs and the top of your hips, and run the tape exactly halfway between them — roughly at navel level. Measure against bare skin, keep the tape horizontal, breathe out normally and do not pull your stomach in. Do not measure over trousers: the waistband does not sit at the right height. Height for this ratio should be your measured height rather than the one on your documents: it changes over time.

Why doesn't this calculator tell me what I should weigh?
Because the formula does not know. The corridor the calculator prints is not a target weight but the width of a band: at 5 ft 10 in, forty-five pounds fit inside normal weight, and the index gives you no way to pick your own number out of them. For a runner or a triathlete what matters is not landing in a band but direction — where weight and body composition have moved over months, under what training load and what sleep. That is a series rather than a single measurement, and no calculator substitutes for it.

Can BMI diagnose anything?
No, and that comes from the sources themselves rather than from caution. The commission convened in 2025 by The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology — 58 experts, endorsed by 76 organisations — put it plainly: BMI-based measures should be used only as a surrogate measure of health risk at a population level, and they give inadequate information about health at the individual level. To confirm excess body fat the commission requires either a direct measurement of fat or at least one additional anthropometric measure alongside the index. This calculator diagnoses nothing: it shows two numbers and says honestly where each one goes blind. If weight is changing on its own, with no change in food or training, give it a couple of weeks and see whether it comes back; if it doesn't, or something else joins it, from sleep to race results, see a doctor.
